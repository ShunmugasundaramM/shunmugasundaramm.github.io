07 · Design System

A small kit, used the same way on every screen.

Ten screens do not need a large system. They need one typeface, one action colour, and a button that always means the same thing, so the customer never has to work out what is clickable.

08 · Final Design

Ten screens, in order.

09 · Outcome

The waiting was never the customer's fault. It was the process.

7 days → same day

Verification moved from the back office into the browser, so the application no longer waits on couriers or a visit.

23 → 10 screens

Duplicated proofs were removed and single-field screens merged, leaving five named steps.

Visit → IPV

In-person verification became a code held up to the camera, captured and confirmed inside the flow.

10 · Reflection

What my own first version taught me

Moving a process online is not the same as redesigning it. The first version was fully digital and still asked the customer to do the back office's work, because it inherited the shape of the paper checklist instead of questioning it.

The competitor research changed the question I was asking. Instead of how to lay out twenty-three screens well, it became which of those screens needed to exist. Once verification was treated as something the system owes the customer rather than something the customer owes the system, the flow fitted into five steps.

Ask once.
Verify immediately.
Never ask again.
