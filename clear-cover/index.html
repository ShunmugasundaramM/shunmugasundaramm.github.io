The Problem

A category that never learned to sell itself

Commercial insurance in India is sold, not bought. Nothing about the price is visible until an agent chooses to show it.

Clear Cover wanted the retail model applied to corporate cover. The hard part: a policy is not one product. It is sixteen, and they price on different inputs.

Brand

A mark that says protection without saying shield

Insurance branding in India runs on shields and umbrellas. The Clear Cover mark is a concentric C — a cover drawn as an open ring, not a closed guard. It holds at the 32px it lives at.

Voice
“Tell us about your company.”
“Buying group health insurance for the first time?”
“Know your exact premium.”
Screen titles are questions the buyer would actually ask. Insurer wording is left untouched wherever it is contractual. Everything else is rewritten in plain English.

Flow 01 · Quote

One question per screen until a price exists

Group health is the flagship product and the deepest flow. Three short screens stand between a visitor and a real comparison table.

Step 1 · who you are
Four fields, all required, marked with an asterisk rather than a validation message the buyer has to trigger.
Step 2 · fresh or renewal
A rating question dressed as a conversational one. The two cards carry an icon each so the choice is readable before the label is.
Step 3 · who is covered
Family definition is the single biggest driver of premium, so it is shown as two illustrated options rather than a dropdown of insurance shorthand.
The payoff

Seven real premiums, from seven real insurers

Live premiums sit in the button, so the price is the thing you click. The summary rail stays editable and re-prices the list in place.

Three filters sit above the results because those are the three things buyers actually re-sort on: maternity, pre-existing disease, and average premium per life.

Then close it

Nine legal declarations, and the number that never moves

The declarations screen is the easiest place to lose someone. The wording is fixed by the insurer, so the only levers are typography and structure.

Declarations · insurer wording, kept verbatim
Checkout · contact details masked, premium inclusive of tax
Flow 02 · Configure

Four tabs that answer every question a buyer asks a broker

Picking a quote opens the plan. Everything a broker would read aloud from a PDF sits behind four tabs, with the premium pinned right and recalculating as benefits switch on.

TAB 01 Add more benefits
Each upgrade carries its own illustration, its contractual definition, and the dropdown that sets the limit. Toggling one moves the total premium immediately.
TAB 02 Features included
A flat term-and-value list. No cards, no icons. This tab is scanned for one specific line, so it is optimised for finding, not for browsing.
TAB 03 Network hospital
The tab that closes the sale. Buyers want to know their own hospital is on the list, so it opens as a city-scoped search rather than a national directory.
TAB 04 Exact premium
Age banding is where an indicative quote becomes a real one. Two ways in: type eight numbers, or upload the spreadsheet HR already keeps, with the template one click away.
The same frame, another product
Only the rating questions change

Workmen compensation prices on worker categories and salaries; marine on commodity, transport and policy type. Same field styling, same summary rail, same Continue — so a buyer who has learned one product can buy any of the sixteen.

Key Decision

Only four of sixteen can be priced instantly.

The other twelve say so. Directors' liability, cyber and most property lines need a human underwriter. Rather than bury them behind a contact form, they keep the same opening screen and end with a plain statement.

Flow 03 · After the Sale

Two portals
behind one door

HR and employees arrive at the same URL from the same policy email. Rather than two sign-in pages, the role is a segmented control that changes the fields below it.

Sign in · role first, credentials second
Sign up · HR against the policy, employees against a staff code

Enrolment is a table, because that is what HR already lives in

Adding dependants is the one task every mediclaim policy generates, every year. The screen is a spreadsheet the user already understands — inline editing, no modals.

Add opens a row
Not a modal. The new row sits in place with a green tick to commit it.
Save Changes stays greyed
Until an edit exists, so the button doubles as the unsaved-work indicator.
Self cannot be deleted
The employee’s own row is editable only. The delete icon is simply not there.
Reflection

What four weeks bought, and what it cost

Working alone against a fixed date forced one discipline: decide the spine early and refuse to redesign it per product.

The cost was research. The enrolment table is built on the spreadsheet HR already uses, not on watching them use it. That is the first thing I would test.

What I would do next
Bulk enrolment
The upload path exists for pricing but not for administration. HR at a 200-person company will not add rows one at a time.
Claims
The employee portal ends at enrolment. The moment that matters to an employee — filing a claim, tracking it, finding a cashless hospital at 2am — is still a phone number.
Mobile
The buy flow was designed at 1366 for a desktop-first buyer. The employee side has the opposite profile and should be designed phone-first.
